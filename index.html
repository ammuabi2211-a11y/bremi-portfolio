greytHR · B2B HRMS SaaS

How I launched a Performance Management System from scratch and drove 180 paid conversions at greytHR

Senior Product Marketing Manager · First dedicated PMM hire · 6 months

  • Product Launch
  • GTM
  • PLG · Appcues
  • Sales Enablement

The Problem

greytHR was launching a PMS into a crowded HRMS market with three completely different buyers. HR leaders cared about compliance. Managers cared about ease. Employees cared about fairness. One message would not work for any of them.

No PMM function. No playbook. No in-app infrastructure. Without a structured launch, the product risked disappearing into a noisy market.

Strategy

Built full GTM from scratch: ICP, 3 buyer personas, positioning framework, and messaging hierarchy. Multi-channel approach combining in-app PLG, email nurture, webinars, and trade shows. Started with a core information doc, then built persona-specific case studies, battlecards, and objection handling guides.

Execution

  • 10 Appcues hotspot variations for feature discovery
  • 6 announcement banners for launch awareness
  • 30+ slideouts with embedded product videos, help docs, case studies
  • 8+ tooltips at key friction points
  • 3 survey flows for pre-launch research and feedback
See full execution detail
  • 5–6 email nurture sequence over 30 days
  • LinkedIn posts and thought leadership content for each buyer persona
  • Product landing page creation with persona-specific messaging
  • Demo video production embedded in Appcues slideouts
  • Help page documentation and in-app guided walkthroughs
  • WhatsApp broadcast campaigns for existing customer base
  • Live webinars with persona-specific product demos
  • Trade shows generating 30+ on-ground demos
  • Full sales enablement: battlecards, demo scripts, pitch assets

The call I made

Run the launch inside the product first. An HRMS adding a module already has its best audience logged in every day, so I built the entire first phase in Appcues to the installed base, with a different hook per persona: compliance and structure for HR leaders, less admin for managers, fairness for employees. External demand generation came second, once the in-app funnel had proven which message converted.

What I now believe

For an add-on module in a platform business, the installed base is the launch channel, not the afterthought. In-app beat email and paid on cost and on conversion, and the 80% demo-to-trial rate came from buyers who were already inside the product when they saw the message.

What I said no to

One launch page with one message for everyone. Paid acquisition for cold prospects in the first phase. Webinars run as feature tours. And promising capabilities that were on the roadmap rather than in the product, even when it would have made the launch copy stronger.

greytHR · B2B HRMS SaaS

How I built and launched a partner marketplace from zero and drove 2,200+ registrations at greytHR

Senior Product Marketing Manager · First dedicated PMM hire · 2 months onboarding + ongoing GTM

  • Partner Ecosystem
  • Co-Marketing
  • PLG
  • Retention

The Problem

greytHR had a partner marketplace called Unite but almost nothing to show for it. A handful of vendors were loosely listed with no co-marketing, no onboarding process, and no GTM strategy behind them.

Customers had no reason to engage with it. Partners had no reason to join it.

Strategy

Identified and mapped 13 HR and employer need categories: Tax Filing, Background Verification, Corporate Gifting, Insurance, Wellness, Digital Lending, Advance Salary, Flexible Benefits, Recruitment, Relocation, Accounting, and Rewards.

Ran twice-weekly calls with each partner to understand their product and build in-app positioning within greytHR. Combined in-app PLG, webinars, trade shows, and partner co-marketing for launch activation.

Execution

  • Onboarded 25+ ISV partners: ClearTax, SpringVerify, Zaggle Save, HappyLocate and more
  • 8–10 Appcues hotspot variations for marketplace discovery
  • 4–6 announcement banners for launch awareness
  • 50+ slideouts with partner value messaging and activation prompts across relevant greytHR application pages
  • 6–8 tooltips at integration touchpoints
See full execution detail
  • 5+ survey flows for category needs and post-activation feedback
  • Wrote all in-app copy for 25+ partner apps within greytHR
  • Co-branded assets and partner enablement for each flagship partner
  • LinkedIn posts and partner spotlight content for each onboarded app
  • Landing page creation for Unite Marketplace with partner directory
  • Demo videos for key partner integrations embedded in-app
  • Help documentation and setup guides for each partner app
  • WhatsApp campaigns to existing customers announcing new partner integrations

The call I made

Treat the marketplace as a positioning problem before a partnerships problem. Instead of listing whichever vendors had signed, I mapped 13 employer need categories first, then recruited partners to fill them, and wrote all the in-app copy for 25+ partner apps myself so the marketplace spoke in one voice inside greytHR rather than 25 different ones.

What I now believe

Customers adopt categories, not logos. Nobody logs into an HRMS looking for a specific gifting vendor; they have a problem, like corporate gifting at Diwali, and the marketplace has to be organized around that problem. The 68% integration adoption rate came from placement at the moment of need, not from partner marketing.

What I said no to

Letting partners write their own in-app messaging. Co-marketing every partner equally, when five flagship partners drove most of the activation. External paid campaigns for the marketplace, because the goal was activation inside the product, not awareness outside it.

Senior Product Marketing Manager · First dedicated PMM hire · 6 months

  • Product Launch

The Problem

The recruitment software market is one of the most crowded HRMS categories, with established players like Keka owning strong mindshare. greytHR needed to launch Recruit as genuinely differentiated, leading with AI-powered hiring automation and the Lisa pre-screening bot.

Three challenges: convince existing customers to spend more, reach new prospects evaluating standalone ATS tools, and differentiate credibly against incumbents.

Strategy

  • Recruiters: faster time-to-hire, automation, data-driven decisions
  • Hiring Managers: fill roles on time, quality of hire
  • HR Leaders: compliance, analytics, hire-to-retire

Launched on AI differentiation: skill-based shortlisting, the Lisa AI bot, a centralized candidate inbox, calendar integration. Then repositioned mid-launch when the numbers said the market wanted something else. See the call below.

Execution

  • 10 Appcues hotspot variations across ATS pipeline and AI shortlisting
  • 6 announcement banners for launch and upsell prompts
  • 25+ slideouts with persona-tailored messaging and demo videos
  • 8+ tooltips at hiring workflow friction points
  • 3 survey flows for needs assessment and NPS
See full execution detail
  • Live webinars with Lisa AI bot demos
  • Trade show presence with live Recruit demonstrations
  • Battlecards, objection guides, and ROI talking points for sales
  • LinkedIn posts and thought leadership content targeting HR leaders and recruiters
  • Product landing page creation with AI-first messaging
  • Demo video production showcasing Lisa AI bot in action
  • Help documentation and onboarding guides for recruiters, managers, and HR
  • WhatsApp campaigns to existing customers for upsell activation

The call I made

Reposition mid-launch. Recruit went out on AI-optimized hiring, which was the obvious differentiator and the one the product team was proudest of. Leads came in below plan. Within weeks I moved the position to 3x faster hiring plus continuity: candidate to employee with no re-entry across hiring, onboarding, and payroll, the one thing a standalone ATS like Keka's structurally cannot offer. Lead volume recovered and conversion held.

What I now believe

AI is a feature, not a position. Every ATS claims AI screening, so it collapses into parity the moment a buyer compares two vendors. Differentiate on what the incumbent cannot copy because of how their product is built, and let AI be the proof point underneath it.

What I said no to

A feature-by-feature comparison with Keka. Chasing new-logo ATS buyers in paid channels before the installed base had converted. Leading with the Lisa bot as the headline, however demo-friendly it was. And holding the original position out of loyalty to the launch plan.

e-con Systems × NVIDIA · Edge AI · European and US Enterprise Markets

How I co-hosted a webinar with NVIDIA and converted 64% of leads to SQLs at e-con Systems

Product Marketing Manager · Full production ownership · On-camera co-host

  • Co-Marketing
  • Enterprise GTM

The Problem

Engineers evaluating edge AI camera solutions had no clear framework for choosing the right Jetson processor and camera combination for their specific application. This ambiguity was creating long evaluation cycles and delayed purchase decisions.

e-con Systems needed to reach a larger and more qualified audience than their own channels could deliver, with enough credibility to cut through the noise.

Strategy

Positioned the webinar as an education-first product launch co-hosted with NVIDIA. Built the narrative around buyer problems rather than product features: how to select the right camera-processor combination across robotics, industrial automation, medical imaging, and autonomous systems.

Execution

  • Defined objective, identified audience, built full narrative arc
  • Partnered with NVIDIA's Hardware PM for Autonomous Machines as co-host
  • Promoted via email, landing page, LinkedIn Ads, and NVIDIA partner channels
  • Presented live on camera: business problem, Jetson capabilities, camera selection framework, case studies
  • Managed live Q&A with clear CTA for demo requests
See full execution detail
  • Post-event nurture sequence routing high-intent attendees to sales
  • Tracked registrations, attendance, SQL rate, and pipeline

The call I made

Make it a decision tool, not a product demo. The agenda was built around the question engineers were actually stuck on, how to pair the right Jetson processor with the right camera for their application, and the product appeared only as the answer to that question. I also chose to co-host on camera myself rather than hand the session to an engineer, so the narrative stayed on the buyer's problem.

What I now believe

Co-marketing works when the partner's name buys you the audience and your content gives that audience a way to decide. Engineers don't convert on pitches. They convert when you shorten their evaluation, and the 64% lead-to-SQL rate reflects that the attendees arrived with a decision to make and left with a framework for making it.

What I said no to

A feature-walkthrough agenda. Promoting to a broad list to inflate registrations, instead of targeted European and US engineering roles. Multiple CTAs, when one demo request at the end was the only action that mattered.

greytHR · B2B HRMS SaaS

How I built a competitive battlecard program that reduced sales ramp time by 40% at greytHR

Senior Product Marketing Manager · First dedicated PMM hire

  • Competitive Intelligence
  • Sales Enablement
  • Win-Loss Analysis
View Battlecard deck

The Problem

greytHR's sales team was going into competitive deals without a structured playbook. Reps were handling the same objections differently, losing deals they should have won, and spending hours every week researching competitors independently.

Every conversation with a Keka or Darwinbox prospect was a gamble rather than a system.

Strategy

Designed a 10-section battlecard format for live sales conversations: competitor overview, when you'll see them, honest strengths, where greytHR wins, discovery questions, objection handling scripts, competitive traps, customer signals, and elevator pitch.

Delivered to full AE, SDR, and SE org with enablement sessions. Maintained as a living resource via continuous win-loss analysis.

Execution

  • Prioritized 10 HRMS competitors by how often they appeared in win-loss, not by market size. Keka and Darwinbox first.
  • Interviewed reps on lost deals before writing a single card, so the objections were real ones
  • Wrote every card in the format reps use mid-call: one page, scannable, scripted answers
  • Rolled out to the full AE, SDR, and SE org with live enablement sessions and role-plays
  • Kept the cards living: monthly win-loss review and competitor monitoring fed updates
See full execution detail

Competitors covered: Keka, Darwinbox, HROne, Zoho People, ZingHR, PeopleStrong, Spine HR, PeopleWorks, BambooHR, and Pocket HRMS.

Each card followed the same 10-section structure: competitor overview, when you'll see them, honest strengths, where greytHR wins, discovery questions, objection handling scripts, competitive traps, customer signals, pricing notes, and elevator pitch. Delivered as brand-compliant PDF and PowerPoint so reps could use them in calls and in decks.

The call I made

Put the competitor's honest strengths on the card. Most battlecards tell reps the competitor is weak at everything, so reps stop trusting them the first time a prospect says otherwise. Each greytHR card opened with where Keka or Darwinbox is actually better, then where we win and how to steer the conversation there.

What I now believe

A battlecard reps don't trust is a PDF nobody opens. The first metric for sales enablement is credibility with your own sales team, and you earn it by being right about the competitor, including the uncomfortable parts. Win rate follows adoption, and adoption follows trust.

What I said no to

A 30-page competitive deck. Covering all ten competitors in equal depth, when win-loss showed two of them in most deals. Feature matrices as the core artifact, because reps don't win calls with matrices, they win them with the right question at the right moment.

VAV ONE · Al Zaabi Group · GCC-native Enterprise Platform · UAE

How I built the full go-to-market strategy for a pre-launch enterprise platform, from positioning to pricing to a 90-day execution plan

GTM strategy prepared for leadership review · Pre-launch, stealth · 2026

  • Enterprise GTM
  • Positioning
  • Pricing Strategy
  • ICP and Personas
  • Launch Planning
View full GTM plan

The Problem

VAV ONE is an eight-module business platform (HRMS, CRM, Accounting, Mail, WhatsApp, Tasks, Analytics, Engage) built in the UAE and already running live across Al Zaabi Group's 35+ companies. It had never been sold outside the group. No positioning, no price, no ICP, no external references, and a market dominated by Salesforce, SAP, Workday, and Microsoft.

The challenge was to take a product with one internal customer and build a defensible plan to win large GCC conglomerates, with every number reasoned from research rather than measured.

Strategy

Positioned VAV ONE as the GCC-native, complete platform that replaces the foreign multi-vendor stack, rather than trying to out-feature SAP. Turned the name into the narrative: Vision, Ambition, Validation.

Defined a primary ICP of UAE and GCC groups with 1,000 to 5,000+ employees across many entities, narrowed to six priority verticals where fragmentation hurts most, and built a nine-criteria scorecard with trigger events (the January 2027 UAE e-invoicing deadline, ERP renewals, succession, acquisitions) so the ICP is timed, not static.

Chose sales-led growth powered by Al Zaabi's owner-to-owner network and business ecosystem, with ABM and partner co-sell behind it. Land one entity, prove it, expand across the group.

Execution

  • Market sizing: ~60 megaconglomerates, hundreds of large multi-entity groups, an AED 29B GCC enterprise software wallet growing 13 to 18% a year
  • Buyer persona matrix by segment: triggers, decision makers, and deal opportunity for conglomerates, retail, real estate, and trading groups
  • Three-tier competitive map: the foreign stack, global suites (SAP, Oracle, Dynamics, Zoho, Odoo), and regional point players (Bayzat, Darwinbox, Focus)
  • SWOT and a clear strategic call on where to win, what to concede, and who to target
  • Pricing built from research: mapped competitor list prices, modelled the foreign stack cost for a 3,000-person group, set floor and ceiling, then chose per active user, three bundled tiers, penetration strategy
See full execution detail
  • Recommended price points: Core AED 70, Business AED 110, Complete AED 150 per user per month, with volume and early-adopter discounts
  • Four messaging pillars, each with outcome, mechanism, CFO impact, and competitive edge
  • Six-step sales motion, year-one funnel, and a GTM scorecard with entities live as the north star
  • Retention and expansion loops, 90-day execution plan, T-30 to T+30 launch plan, and a six-area risk matrix
  • Closed with six concrete decisions for leadership to approve

The call I made

Price below the floor on purpose, and make entities live the north star instead of ARR. A challenger with no references has to buy its first ones, so the launch price sits under the cheapest credible all-in-one, with a stated plan to raise it toward the value band once three to five groups are live. Every other metric in the scorecard exists to explain why that one number moves.

What I now believe

A plan a CFO can't argue with is more valuable than a plan that sounds ambitious. Every number in this deck is derived and says so. Positioning and pricing for a product with one internal customer are hypotheses, and the job of the first 90 days is to replace them with evidence, not to defend them.

What I said no to

Out-featuring SAP. Product-led or self-serve motions at multi-million-AED deal sizes. Launching GCC-wide before the UAE beachhead was proven. Pricing per headcount, when the daily doers are a third of the employees. And a market-sizing slide built to impress rather than to be checked.

How I work

The Research-to-Revenue Loop

Nine steps, in an order that is itself an argument. Positioning comes before a single asset is written. Sales enablement is its own step, not a launch deliverable, because reps need different tools on a different timeline from buyers. Adoption is separate from launch because in a platform business the launch audience is already inside the product. And measurement is step nine only on paper: it feeds step one, which is why this is a loop and not a checklist.

  1. 01

    Research

    Customer interviews, win-loss analysis, competitive landscape, market sizing, and ICP definition.

    Where teams slip: they research the market and skip the lost deals, which is where the real objections live.

  2. 02

    Customer Insights

    Jobs-to-be-Done mapping, buyer persona development, pain point hierarchy, and decision criteria.

    Where teams slip: personas written from job titles instead of from the moment the buyer feels the pain.

  3. 03

    Positioning

    Category definition, differentiation framework, and positioning statement tested against real buyer language.

    Where teams slip: positioning written after the product ships, then everyone wonders why sales ignores it. Recruit taught me to test it before launch, not after.

  4. 04

    Messaging

    Messaging hierarchy, persona-specific value props, objection handling, and proof point library.

    Where teams slip: one message for every persona. HR leaders, managers, and employees never want the same thing from the same product.

  5. 05

    GTM Planning

    Channel strategy, launch timeline, cross-functional alignment with product, sales, and CS.

    Where teams slip: a channel plan with no attribution model agreed with sales, so nobody can say afterwards what worked.

  6. 06

    Launch

    Email sequences, in-app Appcues flows, webinars, trade shows, paid campaigns, and partner co-marketing.

    Where teams slip: treating in-app as onboarding instead of as the highest-converting launch channel they have.

  7. 07

    Sales Enablement

    Battlecards, demo scripts, objection guides, ROI calculators, and pitch decks the sales team actually uses.

    Where teams slip: battlecards that pretend the competitor has no strengths. Reps stop trusting them after one call.

  8. 08

    Adoption

    In-app onboarding, feature education, lifecycle nudges, and PLG triggers via Appcues and email.

    Where teams slip: declaring the launch done at signup. Most of the value leaks between trial and paid, and between paid and daily use.

  9. 09

    Measurement

    Pipeline contribution, conversion rates, feature adoption, win rates, and ROMI tracked monthly.

    Where teams slip: reporting the numbers and never feeding them back into research. Every win-loss review, every survey flow, every underperforming message goes back into step 01.

Why the loop matters: the Recruit repositioning, the Unite category map, and the battlecard updates all came from step 09 feeding step 01. A framework that ends at measurement is a report. One that loops is a system.
